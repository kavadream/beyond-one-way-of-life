# Before We Begin: Giving Life a Little More Room

Sometimes what traps us is not the absence of a way forward. It is a path we have already taken, gradually becoming the only path we allow ourselves to follow.

Your career must reach a certain level. By a certain age, you must have a certain kind of family. A hobby should ideally earn money, and even rest must prove its usefulness for tomorrow. Taken separately, these demands often have something to recommend them. Piled together, though, they can turn a person's entire life into a project awaiting inspection. The hardest part may not be failing to meet them. Even when you do, you dare not pause, because the next requirement has already arrived.

This book asks how we can recognize that narrowing, and leave room for life to take other shapes amid real responsibilities and constraints. It does not urge everyone to resign, or mistake stability for cowardice. It does not urge everyone to become a recluse, or promise that money problems, caregiving, illness, and unfairness will disappear if we change our thinking. The question is whether a choice suits us, not whether it looks brave enough.

The starting point for these reflections is the *Zhuangzi*. Its immense birds, crooked trees, skilled craftspeople, and disputing figures suddenly make familiar measures seem strange. But this book is neither a chapter-by-chapter commentary on the *Zhuangzi* nor an attempt to turn ancient sayings into workplace techniques. Ancient texts have their own circumstances; modern people have theirs. The two cannot simply be made to coincide. Every move from the old book to the present involves interpretation and choice. We will acknowledge that distance rather than present our arguments as answers Zhuang Zhou wrote in advance for modern readers.

By the *Zhuangzi*, this book means the collection of texts transmitted to us, not a work whose thirty-three chapters necessarily came from one person. Its formation and authorship remain subjects of debate, and even the attribution of the Inner Chapters should not be treated as beyond question.[S8] Brief interpretations here offer prompts for thought. They are not findings from psychological experiments, still less clinical advice. This book does not seek an ancient authority's stamp of approval for modern experience.

You will meet Xia Lan, He Ning, Yu Qi, Zhou He, and Lin Chu. They are neither actual interviewees nor pseudonyms for people in the author's life. The characters, conversations, everyday details, and numbers are all original fiction. They live in the same unnamed city. Some know one another; some merely pass the same small shop. They do not collectively achieve some especially enlightened way of living. Some keep their jobs, some change careers, some remain troubled by other people's judgments, and some learn to change and then regret it. Their disagreements are not all resolved, either.

They are here because it is too easy to say, in the abstract, “Don't compare yourself” or “Be yourself.” The real difficulty lies in a meal, a contract, a ride someone needs, and a bill at the end of the month. Whether someone's thinking has changed depends on what they can do the next time the same situation arises that they did not dare to do before. It also depends on who actually pays for that action.

This book will not try to find the final way you should live. Even if you finish it still living in the same place and doing the same job, something may have loosened: you may no longer read one rejection as a verdict that you have no worth; no longer require every relationship to endorse the same choice; no longer rush to invent a use for an ordinary afternoon.

Giving life a little more room need not begin with going far away. Sometimes it begins with finally being able to say the whole sentence: this is how I am arranging my life for now. There are reasons for it, and shortcomings too. Later, I can change it.

# Chapter 1: The Unspoken Rankings at the Dinner Table

Xia Lan stood outside the restaurant for two minutes before dismissing the messages in her work chat. She was twenty minutes late for her high-school reunion. In the private dining room, someone called her name, someone moved a chair, and a classmate by the window handed her the menu, saying they had just ordered a dish she used to like.

The first half hour was easy. They talked about their PE teacher's accent, and who had borrowed half an eraser and never returned it. Then the conversation naturally turned to the present. Someone ran a whole region, someone owned two shops, and someone's child had just started primary school. Nobody announced a competition or deliberately showed off. They were simply selecting the parts of the last few years that could be told in ten minutes.

When her turn came, Xia Lan said, “Still in projects. Mostly managing delivery now.”

The classmate opposite asked whether she was a director yet.

“Not yet.”

“Soon, though, surely? You've always been so capable.”

There was no malice in it, but suddenly Xia Lan felt her answer was missing something. She added that the company was restructuring and there would probably be new opportunities. In fact, she had not decided whether to apply for the position. It would mean working in another city for long stretches, and for months she had been longing for weekends when she did not have to open her laptop.

Near the end of the meal, an old classmate said he had stayed in their county town, teaching during the week and going fishing by the river with his father at weekends. For a moment Xia Lan felt relieved: at least someone seemed not to have gone farther than she had. She quickly felt ashamed of the thought, then defended herself: everyone compares.

On the ride home, she enlarged the group photo to study people's clothes, positions, and expressions. The classmate who taught in the county town was crouching in the front row, smiling an ordinary smile. The photograph could not tell her whether he was content or disappointed, yet she had already assigned him a score lower than her own.

Comparison had quietly changed its purpose. Other people's experiences might have shown her more possibilities: changing roles, taking on care responsibilities, running a small business. Instead, she compressed those differences into a single line. Once she had put everyone in order, she seemed able to know how well her own life was going. The cost was that everything that could not be ranked disappeared.

A few days later, Xia Lan took some borrowed books back to her friend He Ning. He Ning lived in an old building, with a small kitchen and a few pots of unevenly growing spring onions on the balcony. She had just turned down a better-paid job because it involved late shifts on the other side of the city. Without thinking, Xia Lan asked, “Doesn't that feel like a bit of a waste?”

He Ning did not answer immediately. She poured boiling water into their cups. “It does to me, too. I want to earn more. But I have to take Mum to rehab twice a week, and I can't make that work with the late shifts.”

“Couldn't you hire someone?”

“I could. If there's someone suitable, and the money, and Mum agrees. It's not impossible. I just haven't worked it out yet.”

Xia Lan had meant to praise her for living at her own pace. Now she left that unsaid. He Ning had not risen above money and status. She wanted a higher salary too, and found the trips a nuisance. She had simply chosen a combination she could currently manage among things she could not satisfy all at once.

The discussion of size, ability, and use in “Autumn Waters” suggests a different way of looking: excellence at one task cannot determine a thing's standing in every situation.[S5] Bringing that reminder to the dinner table does not mean all occupations pay the same or all choices have the same consequences. It asks instead: what, exactly, are we comparing, and why have we let it stand for a whole person?

What Xia Lan remembered that day was the small patch of sky outside He Ning's kitchen window. The window was small and the buildings stood close together. Seeing it did not suddenly make her yearn for a poetic life of poverty. She simply realized that the titles and incomes she had heard about at the reunion were like that window: they revealed only one part.

Comparison can be very useful when its purpose is to understand conditions. Pay for similar positions can help people judge whether they are undervalued, and other people's experiences can alert them to risks. Later, Xia Lan did ask a classmate about the workload involved in managing from another city. The information helped her understand the promotion more clearly. What she needed was precisely that kind of specific comparison, not a declaration that “I don't compare myself with anyone.”

The difficulty is how easily a limited question expands. A question about fair pay ends as a judgment on whether you have made anything of yourself. Envy of someone else's holiday ends in the conviction that your own last ten years were wasted. Usually there is no evidence for the step between them, only a familiar urgency: you must decide what kind of person you are, quickly.

Two weeks after the reunion, someone in the class chat suggested a trip to the county town. Xia Lan nearly joked that they could try out retirement. Halfway through typing, she deleted it. Instead, she asked whether there was a good stretch of river for an autumn walk. The classmate who taught there sent a map marking two junctions, and warned them to arrive early because it got busy at weekends.

They did not discuss who was happier. Xia Lan still wanted a promotion, and other people's updates still sometimes made her tense. But this time she had gained a path she could actually walk, rather than another set of rankings.

That evening, looking through the reunion photographs, she noticed the man in the front row holding a little bag of takeaway pastries. Before, she had seen only that he was crouching lower than the others. She had never noticed that detail.

Leaving comparison behind need not begin with becoming broad-minded. It may begin simply with looking a little more closely.

# Chapter 2: What Is Left After Thirty-Five?

On her thirty-fifth birthday, He Ning received a voice message outside the cake shop. Her aunt said not to get so busy this year that she forgot the important business of her own life: wait a few more years and it would only get harder. There were market sounds in the background. Her aunt was probably thinking about her while buying vegetables.

He Ning put her phone back in her bag. She was not angry, only tired. She knew what “important business” meant, and knew that to her relatives she looked like a procedure still waiting to be completed. She had had a boyfriend and seriously discussed a shared life. Then they discovered how differently they wanted to arrange money and caregiving, and separated. At family meals, that whole process was usually reduced to one sentence: don't be so picky.

Zhou He came over for cake that evening, bringing a pot of mint. Six years younger than He Ning, she had just left a chain store and was about to start at another shop. Sitting on the floor together, He Ning suddenly asked, “Do you think it's a little late for everything now, wanting to change jobs and find a partner?”

“I already feel late,” Zhou He said. “Some of the people I graduated with have bought homes.”

They laughed briefly, then neither kept laughing. That clock, it turned out, did not wait until thirty-five to start ringing. It could always find an age that turned everything before it into preparation and everything after it into making up for lost time.

He Ning had once made a life plan. A certain qualification by twenty-eight; enough saved for a down payment by thirty; a settled family life by thirty-two. At the time, it had not seemed harsh. Like a road many people had taken, it made an unfamiliar future look orderly. Then her mother became unwell, and she moved to a job that made the trips with her easier. A relationship ended. Several necessary expenses depleted her savings. The plan remained pasted into an old notebook, and she began avoiding opening it.

What really embarrassed her was not just that those wishes had gone unfulfilled. She was still judging the life she had actually lived by their original deadlines. There was no suitable box for the hours spent caring, the work she had learned, or the judgment involved in ending an unsuitable relationship. Her days had been full, yet the form showed blanks.

“Wandering Freely” places lives of vastly different kinds together, unsettling the familiar human scale of time.[S1] This cannot abolish deadlines for modern people. Applications close, bodies change, and caregivers grow older. But an ancient fable's expansion and rearrangement of time can help us ask more precisely: where does this deadline come from? Is it a real window of opportunity, or a date added to make a life look orderly?

He Ning later began separating those questions. When she wanted to know the actual entry requirements for a professional qualification, she read the official announcement instead of asking friends whether it was suitable “at my age.” Wanting a partner, she admitted that desire without treating every new acquaintance as an exam to be handed in before a birthday. On questions of health and childbearing, she knew she needed advice suited to her circumstances from appropriate professionals, rather than a philosophy book declaring there was still time for everything.

Those distinctions did not spare her regret. Some opportunities really had passed. Some losses resulted from choices; some were never hers to decide. But acknowledging a particular loss came closer to her actual circumstances than declaring herself late for the rest of her life. Losing one possibility did not mean every future had to be treated as second-best.

One Saturday, while her mother exercised at the rehabilitation centre, He Ning sat in the corridor reading an old magazine. A woman in her sixties nearby was practising taking photographs on her phone, repeatedly putting a finger over the lens. He Ning showed her twice; the third photo still contained half a finger. The woman did not say she wanted to prove age was just a number. She simply wanted to send her sister a picture of the flowers on the windowsill.

The sight was a relief to He Ning. She had seen so many “never too late” stories whose protagonists started businesses, won awards, or climbed mountains in later life, finally proving through great achievements that age should not limit anyone. Sometimes they encouraged her. Sometimes they added pressure: to justify starting later, she seemed obliged to succeed more spectacularly than everyone else.

The photograph in the corridor had no such assignment. Blurry or not, it was something the woman wanted to do today. It did not have to bring credit to everyone her age.

A few months later, He Ning took a short course in page layout. In the first class she discovered that the student beside her was more than ten years younger. The old thought immediately surfaced: other people are just beginning, and here I am already. Asked to open a file, she found her software menus differed from the projected screen and spent ages finding it. After class, the student beside her asked for help with some instructions, saying she could never explain delivery requirements clearly.

They exchanged a little of what each knew, without becoming an illustration of the strengths of different generations. At home, He Ning added a page to the old plan, listing the things she actually needed to deal with now. Some had definite dates; some wishes had no date for the time being. She did not tear out the old page. It was an arrangement her former self had sincerely believed in.

When her aunt brought up her age again, she still became irritated and did not always answer gracefully. Once she simply said, “I know you're worried for me, and I'm working things out. But I don't want every meal to be spent calculating how many years I've got left.”

The table fell quiet for a moment. Nobody immediately agreed, and her aunt turned away to serve soup. He Ning felt a little guilty and a little relieved. She had not won a battle of ideas. She had merely reclaimed a stretch of that meal from the countdown.

# Chapter 3: The Chair That Never Went Up for Sale

There was a chair in Yu Qi's repair shop with a slightly crooked back and mismatched armrests. After repairing it, he had meant to sell it, but never put a price on it. Neighbours bringing things for repair sat there while they waited. In hot weather, some came in just for a drink of water, with nothing to mend.

His son came to help photograph the old items they could sell online. Seeing the chair, he asked what it would fetch. Yu Qi said he did not know and had not thought of selling it. His son tapped an armrest. “If you're not selling it, it's taking up space. Look how small this shop is.”

He was right. Two more machines near the doorway would leave no room to turn around, and Yu Qi often complained about the rent. The chair's space could certainly hold something else awaiting repair. Yet after his son left, he put it back by the window. That afternoon, an older man who brought in a fan sat for twenty minutes, talking at length about his building's lift. Yu Qi hardly got a word in. Only after seeing him out did he notice that his earlier irritation had eased.

It would be easy to defend the chair by saying it kept customers longer, built trust, and ultimately brought in business. That account would not necessarily be wholly wrong, but it would return the chair to the same ledger: it must prove it will earn more tomorrow to deserve its place today.

The *Zhuangzi*'s stories about trees make “uselessness” a powerful challenge to our assumptions.[S2] The question this book draws from them is not how to become useless, but who decides that something may serve only one purpose. If people can explain their worth only by how much they can be used, employed, or exchanged, even rest and friendship can come to require an earnings forecast.

Yu Qi did not intend to turn his repair shop into a philosophical experiment. Suppliers needed paying on time, and his hours needed a price. A few years earlier, he had repaired so many things free for acquaintances that by month's end he could barely bring himself to buy his own tools. After that, he learned to state the price first. If someone found it too expensive, he no longer proved his kindness by making a loss.

So when his son brought up the chair again, he did not say money was unimportant. He took out the accounts, and they first worked out which monthly jobs made money and which merely kept him busy at a loss. The chair still took up space. But beside several scrap machines that should have gone long ago, it was not the biggest business problem. His son later helped remove two machines. The chair stayed.

Reading “uselessness” as permission not to take responsibility for one's life would be equally careless. The tree and goose episode in “The Mountain Tree” makes a simple formula for self-preservation difficult to sustain: in different circumstances, what is called useful or useless can bring opposite consequences.[S3] Hearing an appealing fable does not entitle us to conclude that life will automatically protect us if we neither compete nor stand out.

Zhou He had sat in that chair. She brought in a broken tape recorder she had used for singing practice. After examining it, Yu Qi said the repair would cost more than an ordinary new machine. She hesitated, then decided to have it repaired. Asked whether it had sentimental value, she said not especially. She simply liked the sound the buttons made when pressed.

At home, Zhou He put it on her desk and recorded two songs. A friend heard them and suggested posting online: put serious effort into it and it might take off. She tried a few times and began paying attention to titles and play counts. Then a song she did not particularly like attracted more responses, and she recorded several similar versions. One day after work, she wanted to sing a different song, but first asked herself: does anyone want to hear it?

She realized she had moved something she loved behind another counter, waiting for customers to order before she opened her mouth.

She did not renounce public performance, or believe earning money from music sullied it. She simply made two folders for recordings: one for other people to hear, the other with no intended destination. Sometimes files moved between them, but that was no longer the compulsory exit for every sound she made.

Of course a person can turn a hobby into a profession. But if every hobby must make that conversion, things offering only ordinary pleasure are discarded one by one like substandard products. Eventually, a person may have a highly efficient life yet be unable to say how they would like to spend an hour when there is no need to perform.

Later, Yu Qi's chair really did break once. A customer heard a noise on sitting down and quickly stood up. His son happened to be there and joked that it had finally proved it was not worth keeping. Yu Qi turned it over, found a loose old screw, replaced it, and carried on using it.

He also knew he might still have to sell it if the shop ever became unsustainable. Keeping something in life that is not priced in money does not mean promising never to change. What matters is that necessary change need not also declare all those afternoons that earned nothing meaningless.

That winter, new opening hours appeared on the shop door. Yu Qi kept half a day each week free of jobs. Zhou He, who had brought the recorder, sometimes passed in the early evening. She would sit awhile and hum a few lines she had not yet learned properly. Some people listened; some did not hear.

The chair still had no price. It did not become a trademark or suddenly make the shop prosper. It simply ensured that inside this little shop there was always a place where someone could sit down first, then decide whether to talk business.

# Chapter 4: Introducing Yourself Without a Work Badge

On her last day at her old company, Xia Lan put her work badge in a paper bag. She had worked there eleven years. Swiping through the gate for the final time did not make her want to cry. At the metro station, though, she suddenly realized she would not be taking this route tomorrow morning.

The promotion she had imagined never came. After a departmental merger, her position was eliminated. The company offered a role in another city. She thought about it for three weeks, then declined. Her decision involved both choice and circumstances beyond her control. It could not neatly be called a carefree resignation, much less explained entirely as lack of ability.

Still, when people asked, she wanted a sentence that sounded complete enough, and good enough. “Taking a break” might seem evasive; “looking at new opportunities” sounded like an unfinished advertisement for herself. For a while, she avoided gatherings where introductions were required and seldom opened her old industry chats.

What she felt first after losing the job was not free time, but the loss of an identity that required no explanation. Before, naming her company and role told people roughly how to treat her. Now she had to explain herself, and discovered she had less to say than she had imagined.

One day she went to Yu Qi's shop to collect a repaired desk lamp. Busy on the phone, he asked her to look through the slips on the counter for a customer's collection date. She quickly sorted out several conflicting entries, then pointed out two items listed at different prices on the same slip. Yu Qi said that without her, he would have spent the evening calling to explain.

Xia Lan felt a moment of pleasure, then disdained the pleasure itself. She had managed projects with budgets exceeding a million yuan. How could praise over a few repair slips make her so happy now? The familiar judgment arrived at once: was this a step backwards?

Her abilities had not expired with her work badge. But appearing in a different setting, they seemed less respectable than before. She saw that she missed not only the work itself but the lustre the job had given those abilities.

The questioning of names, judgments, and perspectives in “On the Equality of Things” can make us notice how terms such as “successful person” or “someone left behind” often freeze particular circumstances into an identity.[S4] This does not make labels meaningless. Contractual roles, professional qualifications, and areas of responsibility matter. Caution is needed when a noun shifts from a description that helps us work together to a verdict on someone's entire life.

Xia Lan began trying to describe more specifically what she was doing. When asked, she said she was looking for work in delivery management and considering smaller companies with shorter commutes. If people asked why not a large company, she admitted she had not worked it out yet. It sounded less polished, but made no unnecessary promises about the future.

She could not always bear their expressions. Once an acquaintance said, “But weren't things going well for you before?” She stayed angry for a long time after getting home, repeatedly composing retorts in her head. Eventually she found what she really wanted to say: I haven't figured things out yet. Please don't be so quick to divide my life into good and bad.

Other people might not wait. Society needs quick categories, and application forms leave little room for a complicated person. Loosening an identity does not depend on everyone becoming considerate. Xia Lan still had to revise her CV, explain the gap, and risk rejection. What she needed to learn was how, while dealing with those categories, to keep some understanding of herself beyond their jurisdiction.

At first that understanding was slight. She liked bringing order to confusion, was willing to take responsibility for deliveries she had promised, and could hear different people's requirements under pressure. She also feared losing control, sometimes treated other people's mistakes as threats, and occasionally refused to ask questions in order to appear professional. Those descriptions sounded less impressive than a title, but came closer to experience she could still use than “former candidate for project director.”

One evening she and He Ning cooked together. As they chopped vegetables, He Ning asked why she always cut carrots to the same size. Xia Lan said they cooked evenly that way. He Ning laughed that she managed delivery even while cooking. Xia Lan laughed too. Once that would have pleased her, proof that she was professional everywhere. This time, looking at several pieces she had deliberately cut larger, she decided not to redo them.

There is no need to abolish a professional identity or belittle past achievements to look free. Eleven years of work had genuinely shaped Xia Lan. She could not and did not want to erase them. The change was that she no longer required that identity to occupy every room. In the kitchen, she could be an inexperienced cook. On a walk, she need not analyse the route. With friends, she need not always solve the problem.

Later, a small company invited her for an interview. The interviewer asked, “Would you be comfortable in a role that wasn't management?”

She neither immediately said yes nor immediately defended herself. She asked what the job involved, who made decisions, and how delivery would be assessed. It was a question about a position, answerable with information about that position. It did not need to become a matter of dignity at once.

On the metro ride home, she took her old work badge out of her wallet. Later she put it in a drawer with old photographs. It was still worth keeping. It simply no longer needed to go everywhere with her.

# Chapter 5: Changing Careers Does Not Mean Starting from Zero

Zhou He arrived before six for her first trial shift at a bakery. The door was still locked. She stood beneath a streetlight eating a steamed bun she had brought from home. At twenty-nine, after working tills and arranging shifts in shops, she wanted to move into food production. The recruiter asked about experience. She said she had no formal training but was willing to start with the basics.

“Then think of yourself as a beginner. Don't bring your old habits with you.”

That reassured her a little. A beginner was allowed not to know, and perhaps all her earlier exhaustion could be wiped away too. She even rather liked “starting from zero,” as if she could finally get a sheet of paper with no mistakes on it.

Once she began, the paper was not blank. Long hours standing tired her quickly, her former routine made early mornings difficult, and lower pay did not bring lower rent. She also brought abilities she had already acquired: spotting staffing gaps at busy times, knowing when a queue would test customers' patience, keeping calm amid confusion. She was clumsy when the baker taught her to shape dough. But when orders backed up at the counter, she put them in order faster than several long-serving employees.

Starting from zero is a convenient phrase that obscures the actual shape of change. People enter a new life carrying bodies, bills, relationships, skills, and old habits. Some things need to go; some deserve to stay; some only reveal their uses later.

On her third trial day, Zhou He spoiled a tray. Instinctively she blamed an unstable equipment temperature. The baker told her to check the production record, and she admitted she had missed a step. At home that evening, she messaged He Ning that perhaps she simply was not suited to this work. He Ning asked which part she was unsuited to. Looking at the screen, Zhou He could not answer. Perhaps what she disliked was merely becoming a novice after being experienced, unable to preserve her dignity with a single confident sentence.

“Autumn Waters” considers how different tools and abilities suit different tasks.[S5] Applying this to a career change is this book's modern extension: proficiency in one environment does not automatically carry over to another; awkwardness in the new environment does not prove that all previous experience has been erased. Neither side should be overstated.

Hearing she wanted to bake, Yu Qi invited her to the shop for tea. In his youth he had maintained factory equipment. When the factory moved, he did not want to follow, and began repairing household appliances. Many said he need not worry because he had a trade. He knew the real difficulties had been setting a price on his own for the first time, dealing with the first non-paying customer, and discovering that knowing how to repair things did not mean knowing how to run a shop.

“The hardest part of changing trades,” he said, “was having to make all those decisions that other people used to make for me.”

Zhou He had wanted only to learn to make the products. After hearing him, she began asking the owner less romantic questions: how the probation period worked, whether the role included counter service, how days off rotated, and who decided the pace of training. She found that she liked making things, but not necessarily every job attached to it. She also saw that without asking, a “brave career change” could simply mean letting someone else define the unknown for her.

Two months later, the bakery reduced staffing and did not keep her on. Business arrangements played a part, as did her continuing lack of speed. For a while she wanted to describe it as proactively looking for a better fit. Finally she told her friends, “They didn't keep me. I still want to do it, but I need another way to learn.”

She found another retail job with steadier hours, regained some income, and practised in part of her time off. Some said she was back where she started; sometimes she felt so too. But she now knew the daily work was more repetitive than she had imagined, that particular stages genuinely interested her, and that she could not currently afford a long period on low pay. Those discoveries did not improve the appearance of her CV, but her next step no longer depended entirely on imagination.

Change can take the form of going back. Returning to familiar work need not prove the new wish was mistaken. A person may be rearranging what supports it. Yet not every retreat must be described as preparation for a greater success. Even if she ultimately stopped, the experience could remain a genuine attempt. It need not await a future award to be spared shame.

One rainy day, Zhou He went back to buy something at the bakery. She had meant to take a detour, afraid someone she knew would ask how things were going. Instead she went in. The baker recognized her and asked what she was doing. Back at work, she said, practising occasionally. He nodded and reminded her to watch the moisture in the dough she had last worked on. Neither consolation nor sarcasm: a specific detail reconnected her to her interrupted learning.

She bought two buns and ate one at the bus stop. It was better than her own, especially the crumb. She examined it carefully for a while. For the first time, she did not rush to interpret the difference as a verdict from fate.

The freedom in changing careers does not lie in clearing away your entire past, but in deciding again which parts should come with you. Zhou He was still working in retail, still singing, and would later bake some bread too. She did not know what occupation these activities might eventually form.

She scheduled her next practice session for an afternoon when she was actually free, rather than some faraway place called “a whole new life.”

# Chapter 6: Finding a Place for Other People's Opinions

Lin Chu was forty-one, a secondary-school teacher. After an observed lesson, she received several pieces of feedback. Most praised the lesson's structure. Just one observer wrote, “The teacher's personal style is somewhat conservative; the classroom lacks vitality.”

That night she could recall none of the praise. Only “lacks vitality” kept repeating in her head. She watched the lesson recording three times and even began disliking the way she walked. Next day she deliberately raised her voice and added more interaction. Instead, the students struggled to follow. After class, one child asked whether she was in a hurry today.

Lin Chu had thought maturity would make her less concerned with judgments. She had told younger colleagues to use their own judgment too. But once an assessment landed on her, that principle was like ill-fitting clothing: wearing it only made her more uncomfortable.

She tried countering it with another voice. Sending a friend the video, she asked whether it was terrible. The friend said it was fine. She then explained her concerns until the friend reassured her repeatedly. After a brief respite she still wanted to ask someone else. She was not collecting opinions. She was searching for a permanently valid licence to go on trusting herself.

A passage in “Wandering Freely” describes Song Rongzi's attitude toward widespread praise and criticism. Yet the text does not present that position as the whole, final answer.[S1] Reducing it to a maxim about “not caring what others think” can easily create another test for ordinary people: if criticism hurts you, you are not enlightened enough.

Nor did Lin Chu need to become immediately unmoved. The feedback might contain something worth hearing, and feeling hurt was no disgrace. The problem was how a general remark had grown from work material into a voice everywhere, influencing even which shoes she put on in the morning.

A few days later, she sent the observer a specific question: which part of the lesson had lacked vitality, and in what learning activities did he want students to be more active? He replied that there had been too little student presentation in the final ten minutes, and suggested more group discussion. Rewatching, Lin Chu admitted that section was rushed. But she did not believe more interaction of every kind would necessarily help. She decided to adjust the final task, without remaking the whole lesson, still less herself.

Once judgments become specific, it is easier to put them where they belong. A comment about one lesson can go into the lesson plan; an opinion based on personal preference can be kept without being followed; circumstances the observer does not know can be explained. Hardest to handle are judgments that refuse to give their grounds yet demand supreme authority. They often begin with “I'm only thinking of your own good” and end with “That's just the kind of person you are.”

After Lin Chu's divorce, someone had called her domineering. Asked for an example, they cited her request for a clearer division of housework. This differed from the lesson feedback. Behind it lay expectations about relationships, not a technical matter she could simply adjust. If she withdrew boundaries she had already discussed carefully in order to be praised as gentle, the judgment would cease to be merely an opinion and become the person making her decisions.

That did not mean she had been faultless in the relationship. She had sometimes jumped to conclusions and turned fatigue into cutting remarks. But taking responsibility for specific mistakes differs from accepting a bad label that explains every conflict. The former allows improvement. The latter leaves a person endlessly working to prove they are not bad.

Over Sunday dinner at He Ning's, she mentioned her recent habit of repeatedly checking feedback. He Ning said, “When people say I'm slow, I want to speed up. But when I do, someone says I haven't thought things through.”

“Sometimes I even fill in things people haven't said,” Xia Lan added.

They laughed. There was no brilliant solution in the laughter, but Lin Chu noticed that judgment did not always come from someone actually standing at the door. Some voices were things heard long ago, some were imagined looks, and some were harsh demands she made of herself in other people's names.

She did not impose a magical no-checking interval on her phone. The next time she wanted to reread the feedback, she simply first asked what she was trying to solve. If she was still revising the last ten minutes, she opened the video. If she merely wanted reassurance that she had not failed, she acknowledged feeling unsettled and did something else ordinary. Sometimes she managed it. Sometimes she clicked anyway.

Outside judgments can also affect income and opportunity. It is careless to say we should ignore them. Lin Chu still prepared carefully for formal assessments and explained inaccurate records. Giving judgments a defined place does not deny their power. It prevents that power from quietly crossing a boundary, turning a work assessment into an order to obey over every meal of the day.

At term's end, she taught another observed lesson. The feedback was better, and naturally she was pleased. On her way home, she wanted to send it straight to every friend who had reassured her. Instead, she sent just one interesting classroom discussion. She found she would rather talk about how the students had overturned an earlier conjecture.

Praise still pleased her; criticism could still keep her awake. But what was happening inside the classroom began taking up more room again than the scoring outside it. The voice had not entirely vanished. It simply no longer needed to live in every room of her home.

# Chapter 7: Not Every Argument Has to Be a Duel

An empty downstairs room was to become a shared activity room, and the neighbours held a small meeting. Some wanted table-tennis tables, some wanted a reading space for children, and others, worried about noise, thought it should remain locked. Yu Qi was asked for his views as someone using a nearby shop. Xia Lan came along to take notes.

At first they discussed hours and uses. Twenty minutes later, they were discussing people. Those wanting table tennis were accused of disregarding older residents; those worried about noise opposed everything; those suggesting reading were suspected of caring only about their own children. Everyone could tell a perfectly reasonable story in which the obstacle happened to be other people's narrow-mindedness.

Instinctively Xia Lan wanted to make a table and sort the views into categories. She had barely begun when a neighbour asked, “Whose side are you on?”

She paused. The question split matters that could be considered together into two sides. As if admitting noise was a concern meant she could not want the room open; as if enjoying a game necessarily meant opposing somewhere for children to read.

Back home, Xia Lan was still angry. She drafted a long explanation in the group chat. By its end she was starting to prove that she was more rational than those people. Suddenly she realized she too was creating a ranking of the participants, simply putting “rationality” at the top.

“On the Equality of Things” repeatedly interweaves opposing positions and judgments of right and wrong.[S4] It does more than give one side a strong argument. It can make us wonder why the ground on which we stand has somehow become the position that needs no explanation. Applying this reading to modern disputes does not abandon facts or make harm and responsibility matters of personal feeling. It reminds us to examine, alongside the facts, the premises slipped quietly into the question.

In the activity-room discussion, some assumed people not at work during the day had more spare time. Others assumed children's activities necessarily mattered more than adults' recreation, or that a public space should be usable at any hour. Undiscussed, those assumptions nevertheless determined how everyone judged everyone else. Hidden from view, they made every disagreement look like a moral flaw.

Before the second meeting, Xia Lan did not prepare a more persuasive speech. With the neighbour coordinating the discussions, she first asked specific questions: when nearby residents rested, how well the doors and windows blocked sound, who could open and lock up, and where problems during a trial should be reported. Rather than prove which group most deserved consideration, she identified where different uses actually collided.

The meeting was still difficult. Some rejected time limits; some thought even a trial would cause trouble. They reached only a very limited arrangement: open at a few times initially, install no equipment likely to cause sustained noise, and keep a separate record of usage. The table-tennis enthusiasts were unhappy, and Xia Lan thought the plan cautious. Still, the room could finally open, and the disagreement retained a way back to revision.

Not treating an argument as a duel sometimes yields no more elegant consensus, only a temporarily unsatisfactory way to keep living together.

Another disagreement arose between Lin Chu and her younger brother over whether their mother should move in with family. Her brother felt proximity to the hospital would be reassuring; Lin Chu worried that leaving familiar neighbours would be hard for her mother. Increasingly, they seemed to be competing over who was the more dutiful child. Then their mother said they had talked for half an hour without asking what frightened her most.

What she feared most was neither moving nor living alone. It was being asked where she was going every time she went out, life becoming a continuous inspection. Her answer did not settle the housing arrangement, but changed the question. Previously they had argued over who understood safety best. Now they needed to discuss concrete arrangements that balanced necessary help with independent living.

An onlooker can easily draw the lesson “listen more to the person concerned.” The difficult part is accepting, after investing so much care, time, and hurt, that the person's answer may not prove you right. Lin Chu continued to worry, and her brother did not entirely agree with their mother. But they no longer used the intensity of their own anxiety as grounds for giving their opinion priority.

Some arguments cannot be resolved by shifting perspective. Debts need verification, violence needs stopping, and unfair arrangements may require an appeal. When one party has substantially more power yet tells the weaker one to stop distinguishing right from wrong, supposed broad-mindedness becomes a way to silence them. The easing discussed here means not tying personal dignity to every victory and defeat. It does not mean surrendering the ability to judge harm, honour commitments, or maintain boundaries.

After the room opened, Yu Qi went one day to repair a loose table leg. The neighbour who had opposed the opening most strongly was there too, wiping a windowsill. Asked what he thought now, he said he still worried about future noise, but since the room was open, he might as well clean the windows first.

He had not become a supporter or conceded defeat. When Xia Lan heard the story, she found herself liking that incomplete outcome. Someone could still disagree while doing something that helped shared life continue.

She deleted the unsent essay on her phone. Not because it contained no valid points, but because it was no longer what the next step needed most.

# Chapter 8: People Who Cannot Go Far Need Room Too

One sleepless night, He Ning watched a travel video. On screen, someone had quit their job and was driving along the coast, eating wherever they happened to stop. She watched, absorbed, until her mother called from the next room: the glass of water beside her bed had tipped over.

He Ning wiped the floor and helped her mother change her clothes. By the time she picked up the phone again, the traveller had reached another city. Suddenly she wanted to cry. Spilled water was not a big thing. But she realized she had to be ready for interruption even while watching a kind of freedom.

Next day she told friends that once things at home were settled, she would go away for a month. She had said it for years, and the month remained in the future. Some urged her not to trap herself; others said caring for parents was a blessing. She could bear neither: the first seemed to say she stayed because she lacked courage; the second denied even her exhaustion a place.

Constraints differ in weight. Some come from habit, some from money, some from people who genuinely need care. Calling all of them “prisons of the mind” places another burden on those most in need of help: not only are their circumstances difficult, but they have failed to think their way out. A book about freedom that misses this can easily become useful only to those who already have room to move.

Flight in “Wandering Freely” also involves the conditions that support it.[S1] From here this book considers how modern people's scope for action needs support too. But the chapter cannot simply be converted into a resource-management manual. The ancient text prompts a question: when we praise someone's freedom, do we see what makes their departure possible, and who stays behind to take care of things?

He Ning truly began changing the arrangements after losing her temper. Her mother asked why she had bought the wrong vegetables. She slammed the bag onto the counter and said she was not a machine. Her mother fell silent, and immediately He Ning felt terrible. She apologized, ready as usual to explain the incident as her own lack of patience.

This time she paused. Patience was needed, but so was a schedule. She contacted her younger brother. Instead of vaguely asking him to care more, she listed regular trips and times for company, and asked which he could reliably take on. At first he said work was busy. She did not turn it into a competition over conscience. She said she worked too, and the present system could not depend forever on her rearranging everything at short notice.

Negotiation was difficult. He lived far away and genuinely could not make some times; their mother was not used to his accompanying her either. After several adjustments they freed only two fixed half-days. He Ning had wanted a month and received something small. She was still disappointed. But those half-days were finally a known arrangement shared by everyone, rather than exceptions bestowed on her when others happened to be free.

The first time her brother took over, she caught a bus to the river. Half an hour later she wanted to go back, worrying that they could not find things, worrying that her mother was unhappy. She called twice. On the second call her brother said, “Everything's here. You get on with what you're doing.”

She had nothing to get on with. That made her uncomfortable. Previously, she had always left home for work, shopping, or an errand; leaving itself required a legitimate reason. Now she simply sat, bought a fairly expensive hot drink, and watched people pushing carts past. Later she thought the hardest part that day had not been going out, but refraining from handing the freed time straight to another responsibility.

Her mother needed space of her own too. She told He Ning there were several things she could actually do herself, but her daughter, afraid something would go wrong, always did them first. He Ning initially resisted: she was so tired, and now she was interfering too? Together they distinguished tasks genuinely needing help from tasks merely done more slowly. A statement about respecting autonomy could not complete that process. It required continuing adjustment to actual abilities, surroundings, and professional advice.

Gradually He Ning saw that both caregiver and care recipient could become fixed in their roles: one must always be useful, the other must always prove a need for help. If a relationship allowed only those two positions, both would become exhausted. Later they kept an ordinary habit: listening to a short piece of traditional opera after dinner, without using the time to ask about symptoms or discuss tomorrow's schedule. Sometimes they still could not resist those topics, but at least they knew there were other things to talk about.

This did not turn He Ning into someone who could leave on a whim. Unexpected events sometimes cancelled even the half-days, and money and physical circumstances still constrained her plans. Writing about these changes must not exaggerate a little breathing room into an adequate substitute for real support. Care services, reasonable working arrangements, and affordable resources cannot be replaced by an individual's state of mind.

Yet within existing circumstances, a little space that can actually be secured still matters. It let He Ning allow herself an afternoon without waiting for all responsibilities to end forever. It also helped her state what she needed more clearly, instead of silently waiting for someone to notice her exhaustion.

Later she saved the coastal travel video. She did not delete it, but no longer opened it daily. She still wanted to go. She simply stopped treating a distant journey as the only proof that she had lived.

That Wednesday she saw a small boat moored by the river. Nobody rowed it or explained how far it ought to go. She sat until the water in her cup was no longer hot, then walked slowly toward the bus stop.

# Chapter 9: Ease Does Not Mean Effortlessness

A young apprentice named A-Cheng joined the repair shop. On his first day he watched Yu Qi dismantle an old machine. The movements seemed straightforward: remove a few screws, inspect the wiring, replace a component, and reassemble it. Yu Qi worked for half an hour and charged more than A-Cheng earned in half a day of casual labour.

“Once you know how,” A-Cheng asked, “is it always this easy?”

“Sometimes. Sometimes I don't dare take the job.”

A-Cheng thought he was being modest. A few days later, another machine arrived that looked similar on the outside. He dismantled it from memory and found the internal structure completely different. Yu Qi told him to stop, establish the model, consult the relevant information, and confirm the safety requirements. A-Cheng felt discouraged, suspecting the master was withholding a faster method.

We often mistake skill for a life that demands no cost. A singer seems to produce a melody casually, a teacher to respond naturally to a student's question, a repairer to see a fault at a glance. The repeated failures, research, and pauses to distinguish one situation from another are usually absent from what we watch. “Going with the flow” can then come to mean that once we find what truly suits us, clumsiness and hard work will disappear.

In the story of Cook Ding in “Nourishing Life,” skill and caution coexist. The text does not stop at effortless-looking movement: when complexity appears, the movements slow.[S6] What this book attends to is sensitivity to a particular situation. It does not treat the story as an efficiency technique or claim it has proved a modern psychological mechanism.

Yu Qi was not Cook Ding and did not wish to perform composure for customers. Once he misdiagnosed a fault and replaced an unnecessary part. He explained it to the customer, recalculated the charge, and absorbed the extra cost himself. A-Cheng asked why he could not include it, since the machine was fixed. Yu Qi said the mistake had been his judgment; he could not make it up by exploiting the customer's lack of knowledge.

Skill includes more than speed. It includes knowing one's judgment may be wrong. If “I already know how” becomes proof that observation is unnecessary, past experience changes from an aid into an obstruction.

Later, Xia Lan took on a short project at a small company. Eager to prove she could adapt quickly, she brought a complete set of procedures from her former job in the first week. Nobody objected in the meeting. In practice, things repeatedly stalled. At the small company, each person filled several roles and many documents had no dedicated owner. Xia Lan thought they lacked proper procedures; they thought she complicated everything.

She had planned another training session, then decided first to work through an actual delivery with her colleagues. From the initial request to final confirmation, she found that some confusion genuinely needed correcting, while some apparently casual methods were workable ways to cooperate with limited resources. Her old procedures were not entirely wrong. They simply could not be transplanted wholesale, demanding that the new setting first become the old one.

She deleted several unnecessary records and retained the key steps affecting responsibility and quality. Afterwards she worried that she seemed less professional: what had taken so long to learn now amounted to one thin page. The project lead, however, said it was finally clear whom to approach next.

Mature skill sometimes reduces the outward forms. But an observer seeing only that final page might think simplification was easy from the start, or required no learning. The difference was that Xia Lan knew what she had removed, and under what circumstances it needed restoring. Simplicity came from discernment, not refusal of complexity.

Zhou He had taken a similar detour in singing practice. She once thought a natural voice should remain unaffected by training. As soon as practice required effort, she suspected she had lost her authenticity. Later she found that the passage expressing her best often emerged only after many adjustments. Conversely, some well-practised techniques got in a song's way: she could not resist inserting them where they were not needed.

She began allowing each practice session to address one small problem, and allowing a song to end without immediately judging whether she had talent as a person. Sometimes, still unhappy after half an hour, she stopped for dinner. Stopping did not mean her technique was perfected. It meant that was where today's time and energy ended.

There was nothing mysterious about these people's ease. It came with working hours, opportunities, and the costs of mistakes, and depended on bodies, tools, and surroundings. Some have ample resources and can practise slowly. Others earn their living with a skill and cannot casually afford failure. Discussing mastery while ignoring those differences, demanding a graceful state of ease from everyone, can create new unfairness.

A few months later, A-Cheng finally completed a simple repair independently. Delighted, he hurried to fit the casing and show Yu Qi. Yu Qi told him to check it again according to the procedure. A-Cheng sighed that it was already fine, but complied. During the check he found an insecure wiring connection, and fixed it before handing the machine over.

That afternoon, Yu Qi did not praise his natural gift. He said only, “You were right to stop this time.”

At first the praise seemed underwhelming. Later, thinking what might have happened had he rushed to hand it over, A-Cheng understood that skill did not mean continual acceleration. It also included room to breathe without needing speed to prove oneself.

Perhaps the composure worth wanting is not a permanent absence of resistance, but the ability, when resistance appears, to see the task in our hands clearly without rushing to use it to prove who we are.

# Chapter 10: You Do Not Have to Make Peace with Loss Right Away

Two years after Lin Chu's father died, an old coat still hung in the family home, behind the door, its cuffs worn shiny. Her mother said it took up space and asked her to take it away. Her brother suggested donating it since nobody wore it. Lin Chu folded it into a bag, reached the doorway, and took it out again.

She knew her father would never wear it again. She knew a coat could not keep a person here. The difficulty was not failure to grasp those facts. The coat gave certain memories somewhere tangible to stay: her father patting his pockets for keys before going out, draping it over her schoolbag in the rain, and, later, walking slowly but insisting on doing up his own zip.

Friends comforted her by saying everyone had to look forward. She nodded, even reassuring them in return that she was fine. Once they left, though, she felt lonelier. As if, to spare others discomfort, she first had to make her grief better behaved.

The story of the death of Zhuangzi's wife in “Perfect Joy” is often reduced to a symbol of philosophical acceptance. But the figure in the text also speaks of initial grief; he is not untouched from the beginning.[S7] Even so, it remains an ancient narrative expressing particular ideas, not an emotional standard that every bereaved person must imitate. This book does not use an attitude in the story to demand that readers hurry their grief along.

When Lin Chu first reread the passage, what helped most was not a conclusion that she “ought to let go,” but a smaller discovery: change need not erase earlier feelings. Being able to speak calmly later does not prove the initial pain was wrong. Still hurting today does not prove the past two years have brought no movement in life.

She took the coat home without giving the act a symbolic name. Later, tidying a cupboard one day, she noticed it had not been taken out for a long time. She kept a spare button, washed the coat, and dealt with it appropriately. She chose no anniversary and posted no essay about letting go that day. A few weeks later, she cried because she could no longer find it.

That reversal did not invalidate the earlier decision. She was encountering the same loss in different ways on different days.

Loss can also change how people understand themselves. While her father lived, Lin Chu could be called home at any moment to fix his phone, move a flowerpot, or listen to old stories. When he died, those little tasks disappeared too. At first she thought only time had opened up. Later she realized she had also lost an identity: someone needed her in that particular way.

Listening, Xia Lan remembered the mornings after leaving her job, when the work chat fell quiet and nobody awaited her decisions. She did not say the two losses were alike. Their weight, irreversibility, and relationships differed and could not casually be equated. But she began to understand that sometimes people cling not only to someone or something, but to the self called into being within that relationship.

This also helps explain why “you still have so much else” is not always comforting. New relationships, work, and interests may matter greatly, but cannot offset what is lost as if exchanging goods. Acknowledging that replacement is incomplete can spare a new life an impossible assignment.

Lin Chu and her mother remembered differently too. Her mother seldom looked through photographs but liked cooking dishes her father had enjoyed. Lin Chu once thought she was reluctant to talk and worried about suppressed feelings. Her mother said being asked every day made her more tired. Later, Lin Chu learned to ask first whether she wanted to talk today. If not, they chopped vegetables together.

Members of one family may lose the same person without needing identical expressions to prove the depth of their love. Some keep belongings; some sort them quickly. Some tell stories; some are quiet. Provided they do not turn their own way into a measure for everyone else, those differences need not be eliminated.

When distress persistently affects eating, sleeping, or daily life, practical support and professional help may matter. A book can offer company and words, but cannot assess an individual's condition on that basis. Still less can it demand that anyone sustain themselves alone through philosophical reading. Seeking help does not make a person less accepting of life's changes.

One spring two years later, Lin Chu took students on a school trip to the countryside. Passing a row of trees, she suddenly remembered her father correcting a tree name she had mistaken. She took out her phone to send him a picture. Her finger stopped at the contacts list. It took a moment to remember. She stood there hurting for a while, then sent the photograph to her brother, asking whether he recognized the tree.

He answered incorrectly. She laughed and told him their father used to say his memory was poor too. They exchanged a few messages, then returned to what they were doing. The afternoon became neither a solemn commemoration nor a sign of farewell to grief. Their father, no longer able to answer a message, still entered their everyday lives.

Sometimes not being trapped in one way of living means allowing yourself to change. At other times, it means not forcing yourself to change in the direction others expect. Some insist that a good life must always move forward; others insist that maturity requires quick acceptance of impermanence. Both demands can narrow days that are difficult enough already.

Later, Lin Chu put the spare button in a small box with several old tickets. She did not open it every day or swear to keep it forever. It was simply there, like a question that did not need an answer for now.

# Chapter 11: Being Honest About Wanting More

When Xia Lan received a formal offer from the new company, the salary exceeded her short-project earnings, and the responsibility was greater too. They wanted her to build a team and complete several complex deliveries within a year. She read the offer three times, her heart racing. The excitement was familiar, with a little ambition and a little satisfaction at finally being noticed again.

She wanted to tell He Ning immediately but stopped while typing. Recently they had discussed not being defined by work, and she had said she wanted a roomier life. If she now accepted a busier role so happily, would those earlier reflections look like consolation for disappointment?

She realized she had barely loosened one standard of success before nearly entering another. Previously she had to rise to fulfil her potential. Now it seemed she had to be composed, want little, and avoid competing to prove she had truly understood life. Both demanded a consistent image: she could not value leisure today and be drawn to a challenge tomorrow.

After hearing her out, He Ning said, “If you want it, talk through the terms. You don't have to pretend you don't want something to prove you don't care.”

Xia Lan saw that envy and desire did not have to be eradicated as soon as they appeared. She liked bringing complex projects to completion, earning more, and gaining greater decision-making power. Vanity might be mixed into those wishes, but a mixture did not invalidate them all. The more useful question was how much she would pay for each part, and whether someone else would bear costs she had left unspoken.

She discussed terms with the company, asking to minimize last-minute intercity trips, clarify who decided at critical points, and explain the team's current staffing gaps. They did not agree to everything. In the end she accepted some limitations and relinquished one requirement she had initially insisted on. The decision carried risk. Reflection did not guarantee it was right.

Two months into the job, she was coming home late. One day she cancelled at short notice a promised exhibition visit with He Ning. He Ning did not reproach her, only said the tickets had expired. Xia Lan hurried to explain the project's importance, then stopped halfway through. Important work did not automatically make a broken promise costless. She repaid the ticket money and admitted she had planned badly, without demanding that her friend understand everything merely because she was pursuing a career.

Discussion of different ways to live can easily slide into a gentle moral ranking: people in small towns are wiser than people in big cities; slow living is more authentic than being busy; giving up status brings you closer to freedom than continuing to strive. Then we have not acquired more ways of measuring life. We have merely reversed the scale.

The *Zhuangzi*'s different chapters themselves contain complex voices and tensions that cannot be flattened into a modern career position.[S8] This book therefore keeps a caution in view: if reading an old text merely gives us grander language for looking down on another life, the habit that trapped us may not have changed at all.

Yu Qi's son happened to choose the opposite direction. He wanted to work elsewhere, with better pay and a chance to encounter larger operations. Yu Qi had hoped he would stay and turn the shop into a father-and-son business. Hearing his son intended to leave, he said, “What's so good out there? You'll still just be working for somebody else.”

As he said it, Yu Qi knew something was wrong. For years he had hated people casually declaring his small shop had no future. Now, with his son, he wanted to settle the worth of another kind of life in one sentence. His son did not immediately argue. He only asked, “When you didn't want to move with the factory, wasn't that because you wanted to choose for yourself?”

Yu Qi gave no answer then. A few days later, he asked about accommodation and the probation period. He still worried and still wanted someone to take over the shop, but began separating his own wishes from his son's decision. Concern could supply information without becoming a reason to forbid departure.

Of course, people sometimes invoke career ambition while avoiding other responsibilities. A choice of quiet living may also be an attractive name for fear of failure. But those possibilities call for honesty about concrete actions. They do not justify declaring all ambition vain or all quietness cowardly. Another person's outward choice often tells us too little to know all the reasons within it.

Later Xia Lan kept a simple check for herself: did this job still contain the work she wanted to do? Had its costs exceeded what she had originally been willing to bear? Did those affected have a chance to object? The questions would not calculate an answer automatically, but could prevent her from enshrining one decision as an unalterable faith.

Half a year later, she lost out in a competition for a higher position and felt low for days. She did not tell herself that a truly free person would not care, or immediately conclude that the company was unworthy. She admitted she had wanted it and that not getting it hurt. Then she examined which parts of the work still deserved to continue.

Around the same time, Zhou He sang two songs at a small performance. Asked whether her next step was to become a professional singer, she said not for now. Sitting in the audience, Xia Lan felt for the first time that they did not have to move at the same speed to keep understanding each other.

Some want to spend their lives climbing; some want more than anything to put their home in order; some want both, giving different things priority in different years. We can discuss conditions, consequences, and responsibilities without awarding these arrangements a single ranking of personal worth.

Leaving the performance, Xia Lan saw new messages on her phone. She glanced at them but did not answer immediately, first finishing a bowl of hot noodles with Zhou He. She would still work conscientiously tomorrow. The meal did not prove she had transcended her career. It proved only that, for that evening, her career had not occupied the whole table.

# Chapter 12: Letting the People You Love Become Someone Different

Lin Chu chose a course for her niece. She thought the girl expressed herself well, and systematic training would give her more choices later. She carefully compared the content, asked about teaching arrangements, and reserved a trial-class place for her. Her niece was unhappy when told: she already had plans with friends that weekend.

Lin Chu felt hurt. She wanted nothing in return and had not asked for future gratitude. She simply feared the girl would waste an opportunity. Her brother said that her aunt only wanted what was best for her. Her niece stopped arguing and looked down at her phone. Lin Chu mistook the silence for agreement, until the day of the class, when the girl did not appear.

At dinner, Lin Chu declared she would not get involved again. Setting her chopsticks down hard against the bowl, she felt that the worst part was not the wasted place but having her concern treated as unnecessary. She had lodged some of her self-worth in being able to think farther ahead for the family. When the help was not accepted, it seemed her whole person had been rejected.

Later her niece messaged: they could discuss things she wanted to learn together, but please do not sign her up first and then ask whether she would accept the kindness. Lin Chu stared at the message. Her first thought was that the child was getting very good at making arguments. Only the second was that perhaps she really had left little room for a decision.

The fable of Hundun at the end of “Responding to Emperors and Kings” portrays danger in an act of well-intentioned alteration of another being.[S9] It is not a modern family guide and cannot directly adjudicate real disputes. But it can make us hesitate before a very familiar move: assuming that because we think a certain form is good, another life ought to take that form too.

Lin Chu apologized to her niece without turning the apology into another lesson. She asked what the girl had been busy with and learned that she and friends had spent weeks preparing a small craft market. Instinctively she wanted to analyse market demand. Holding back, she first looked through all their photographs.

Not all the pieces were well made, and they might not sell. Respecting someone does not require praising everything. Lin Chu said several pieces were interesting, and admitted she knew little about this sort of market. If they wanted help with the descriptive text, they could send it. She offered help that could be accepted or refused, rather than a takeover in the name of care.

The change was incomplete. The day before the market, she could not resist several reminders about weather and transport. Her niece said they had checked, and Lin Chu wanted to add that young people were never careful enough. Eventually they agreed to send her only what genuinely remained unresolved. She felt a little bereft. She was no longer needed at every stage, but the relationship had also become lighter.

One difficulty in close relationships is that we often love a person and a particular story about them. Parents may love their children and also the imagined stable lives they will lead. Friends may love each other and also the old arrangement in which they could always stay up together and complain about the same things. When someone changes, disappointment does not come only from failure to understand. Sometimes the shared story can no longer continue unchanged.

He Ning had felt this when Xia Lan became busy with work again. While Xia Lan was taking a break they had met weekly; now arranging a time was hard. Once He Ning said, “As soon as you're back at work, you're just like you used to be.”

Xia Lan heard blame; He Ning had meant that she missed her. They exchanged a few angry words and fell silent. Two days later, He Ning explained again: she knew the new job mattered, but did not want to be the one waiting for a last-minute message every time. Xia Lan admitted they could not meet as often as before, but could make plans ahead and avoid casually cancelling. They lost the old convenience of meeting whenever they wished without necessarily losing the friendship.

“Perfect Joy” also uses a story of tending a bird to expose the danger of mistaking one's own preferences for another's needs.[S7] This book applies the reminder to the limits of care: good intentions must engage with someone's actual needs rather than repeat the helper's familiar motions. At the same time, a person is not a specimen of a species with permanently fixed needs. What someone wants today may differ from what they wanted three years ago.

Allowing others to change makes relationships less certain. You may no longer know what line will make them laugh or recognize at a glance what they will choose. Some advice may stop working; some shared habits may end. Getting to know a familiar person again takes more effort than keeping them within an old description, but brings us closer to actually being with them.

This does not require unlimited accommodation. We can still refuse choices that violate our boundaries. Changes in shared responsibilities need renegotiation. Allowing others to become different is not the same as allowing them to exhaust us at will. Lin Chu was willing to let her niece arrange her courses, but unwilling to keep paying for commitments made without discussion. She made that clear too.

After the market, her niece brought her a small handmade tray. The rim was a little crooked, and a small date was written underneath. They had earned little; some pieces had not even covered material costs. But her niece had many specific things to tell: which customer asked a strange question, who forgot change, and how they borrowed a tablecloth at the last minute.

Lin Chu listened without making every error a teachable moment. Later her niece asked for help making the next set of description cards clearer. At that moment, help returned to the relationship, but not as a right automatically conferred by seniority and past effort.

Lin Chu put the tray by the front door for keys. It did not become the refined object she might have preferred, but gradually acquired signs of everyday use. She found she no longer wanted quite so much to correct its slight crookedness.

# Chapter 13: An Afternoon Does Not Owe You Results

Zhou He once planned a perfect day off: exercise in the morning, reading in the afternoon, singing practice in the early evening, tidying at night. She wrote the plan beautifully. Next morning, she woke close to eleven. It was raining outside. She scrolled on her phone in bed, increasingly convinced the day was already wasted.

To salvage it, she hurried to a nearby café with two books and headphones. First she took a photograph, then spent time choosing a caption. By the time she opened a book, she was worrying about fitting in singing practice later. Nobody was hurrying her that afternoon, yet she felt more tense than at work.

On the way home, she passed Yu Qi's shop and saw him at the door peeling a mandarin. She asked why he had so much leisure today. He said the jobs were done and he was waiting for a customer to collect something. She said he knew how to enjoy himself. He smiled and said he had worked late yesterday. Today he was simply tired.

Suddenly she realized she had made even his rest into an accomplishment. As if a person had to be idle in an elegant way to avoid wasting life. Peeling a mandarin had been just that. Under her gaze, it had entered a quality-of-life contest.

Rest today easily acquires assignments. Walking should improve concentration, reading should broaden understanding, and sleep should make tomorrow more efficient. Those effects may exist. But if they become the only permitted reasons to rest, a person never really leaves work, merely moves to another preparation area to continue the shift.

The “use of the useless” in “The Human World” helps this book leave the question of purpose open for a while, rather than invent more concealed returns for every leisure activity.[S2] A walk may certainly bring inspiration. Yet a walk without inspiration need not be judged a failure. Counting everything as an investment can leave only the future entitled to enjoy life, while the present is forever responsible for paying in.

Later Zhou He tried not filling the day before a rest day with plans. The first time she arranged nothing, she felt empty in the afternoon and nearly concluded she was unsuited to idleness. But after handing all her time to external tasks, she might not instantly know what to do with sudden blank space. Not knowing how to rest need not become another character flaw.

She went to the food market intending only to buy dinner, then learned from a stallholder to distinguish two kinds of leafy greens she had confused. When the seller became busy, she moved aside, without turning it into an assignment in observing the city. At home she oversalted the food, and dinner was not a success. The afternoon offered no special reward simply because she had been willing to relax.

This matters. We often make a way of life's value depend on its ability to produce captivating images. A solitary walk should meet a sunset; consuming less should bring tranquillity; leaving competition should bring better sleep. Actual life may offer overcast skies, boredom, and overly salty soup. If we accept only beautiful by-products, relaxation remains a demanding transaction.

Lin Chu had a similar experience. She bought a thick novel, found it dull halfway through, and forced herself onward because she had already paid and read so much. One day she returned it to the shelf and took down a thin old art book. She was not declaring she would never read difficult work again. She simply admitted she did not want to read this particular book that day.

An interest can involve discipline and difficulty. Someone may willingly study an art for years and endure a beginner's frustration; that too is part of freedom. But when every choice within a hobby must demonstrate progress, enjoyment is pushed far away, like a graduation reward that never arrives. Allowing occasional lack of progress does not negate serious commitment.

Of course, not everyone has a whole afternoon to leave blank. People facing excessive hours, unstable income, or intense care responsibilities may first need actual conditions for rest rather than exhortations to relinquish their fixations. Zhou He's days off also depended on the shop's rota allowing them. If managers impose excessive work and offer a “relaxed mindset” as compensation, private attitudes are being used to conceal how work is organized.

Once a shift swap was cancelled at short notice, leaving her exhausted after working continuously. The manager said young people recovered quickly. Instead of silencing herself with “I ought to be tougher,” she asked for the agreed rest to be made up. That was more practical than trying hard to look relaxed in a café. Room in life requires both inward permission and time genuinely freed in the world outside.

A few weeks later, Zhou He finally had an afternoon with no particular plan. She hung out the washing and sat by the window listening to vehicle repairs downstairs. It was not a pleasant sound, so she shut the window. She sang half a song, forgot the remaining words, and did not look them up. Later a friend sent a photograph, and they chatted casually without reaching any conclusion.

That evening she tried to record the day, wrote two lines, and felt there was nothing worth writing. Previously this would have made her anxious, as if not recording it meant it had not happened. This time she closed the notebook and heated leftovers.

The day could prove no grand principle. Tomorrow she might be anxious again and fill another weekend with activities. But at least there had been this afternoon: it produced no work, improved no CV, and did not become a particularly good story.

She lived through it. That did not leave her with any less of a life.

# Chapter 14: You Can Leave Parts of the Map Unfinished

Before winter, they held a small shared meal in the activity room. There was no official theme; everyone brought a dish. Yu Qi carried over several old chairs, Zhou He brought homemade bread, and Lin Chu made a pot of soup. Running late, He Ning called to check there was still a seat. Setting out bowls, Xia Lan said yes, there was no need to rush.

Only after saying it did she remember standing outside her first reunion, carefully adjusting her clothes and deciding how to describe her life. There had been no malice at that table. Much of the pressure had grown later from her own interpretations. Tonight people would talk about work and income too, but Xia Lan was less eager to translate the conversation into rankings.

Yu Qi's son had left to work elsewhere. The shop remained, and succession was unresolved. A-Cheng had learned some jobs but had not decided whether to stay long-term. Sometimes Yu Qi worried that in a few years he would no longer manage the work. His son suggested reducing the range of services, and when he could not bear to listen, he still lost his temper. The chair and half-day off had not solved everything or made him an older man who could always take things philosophically.

He Ning's life had achieved no elegant transformation either. She moved to an employer with more suitable hours, but her income rose only slightly. Care arrangements sometimes failed, and she still argued with her brother. The month-long trip never happened. She first spent two nights in a nearby city. The photographs showed ordinary streets; she did not describe it as being reborn.

Zhou He still worked in a shop. Sometimes her bread turned out well, sometimes not. She had performed a few times; once she sang off the beat and hid for a long time after leaving the stage. Accepting different ways of living had not made her indifferent to her performance. Yet later she still wanted to sing again, without first proving that singing would eventually become a career.

Lin Chu was again called conservative in her teaching. This time she did not lose sleep, although she was displeased. Her niece ran only two markets before becoming busy with other things. Lin Chu did not immediately interpret the change as lack of perseverance. She herself had recently started learning a very complicated soup and, after two failures, did not feel like trying again for now. Gradually she acknowledged that changing direction did not always require a sufficiently grand reason.

Xia Lan was doing well at work, yet still considering whether to continue at the present intensity. A friend asked whether, having proved her ability again, the next step was another promotion. She said she had not decided. Nor did she dress that uncertainty as profound wisdom. She needed the money, liked the work, and was genuinely tired. Complexity was complexity. She could honestly admit that it did not yet resolve into one answer.

Over the meal they talked about their lives. Someone envied Xia Lan's income, someone envied Yu Qi's freedom from a boss's moods. Yu Qi immediately corrected that: customers and landlords were not all easy either. Everyone laughed. Differences had not vanished, and comparison was not forbidden. Each expression of envy had simply begun making room for more facts.

We cannot live entirely outside measures. Being late keeps someone waiting; debts need paying; work has quality requirements; shared tasks require clear responsibilities. A book about escaping confinement that ended with “anything goes” could not accompany readers back into real life. What needs loosening is the erasure of other experiences once one measure acquires unlimited jurisdiction.

Income can tell us what someone can afford, but cannot alone tell us whether they deserve respect. Age can indicate certain practical conditions, but cannot declare a deadline for every wish. Assessments offer clues but should not directly decide whether someone's existence passes muster. Past experience deserves keeping without becoming an order that the future may only repeat it.

These distinctions sound clear and are often hard to sustain. Under pressure, people clutch familiar rulers again. Xia Lan will still feel uneasy after gatherings, He Ning still worry about birthdays, and Yu Qi still compare his shop with other businesses. Not being confined to one way of life may be less a permanent state achieved than the ability to open things up again after discovering they have narrowed once more.

This book has drawn many questions from the *Zhuangzi*, but no blueprint for everyone to live by. The text's differences, transformations, and unsettled meanings encourage this book to leave interpretive space.[S4][S8] That is a contemporary reading choice, not a claim to have exhausted the ancient work. Readers may disagree with some of this book's arrangements, or find a different emphasis in the older text.

Late that night, as people prepared to leave, Zhou He asked whether Yu Qi would take the window chair back. Of course, he said: the shop needed it. As they carried it, someone suggested photographing it and writing about all the people it had kept company. Yu Qi laughed. No hurry, he said. Someone would need to sit in it tomorrow. Don't sum up its whole life yet.

The remark was no more inherently wise than anyone else's. It simply happened to fit. Often we rush to write a conclusion for a choice: was that career change worth it? Were those years wasted? Was leaving the old path courage or retreat? A conclusion reached too early recruits all subsequent experience, requiring every day to testify on behalf of a past decision.

After the meal, Xia Lan and He Ning washed dishes together. He Ning said she had not yet revised her travel plan; Xia Lan said she had not settled what came after the project. Neither urged the other to find an answer quickly. The bowls were greasy, and it took a while under hot water to rinse them clean. Outside the window someone called a child home.

Of course the future needs planning. Some things require prompt attention; keeping possibilities open cannot excuse delaying responsibilities. But a plan can state today's reasons while admitting that information will change. It can be followed seriously for a time while keeping conditions under which it can change. It need not pretend to define the self in a way that will remain right forever.

Before leaving, He Ning asked when they would meet again. Xia Lan said they should first see when everyone was free. Nobody required the meal to become a tradition that must be fulfilled every month.

Yu Qi carried the chair back to the shop, Zhou He hurried for the bus with leftover bread, and Lin Chu called her mother. Xia Lan waited at the junction for a green light, remembering several difficult problems awaiting her tomorrow. She had not suddenly become light-hearted or received any guarantees.

But she knew those problems needed handling one by one, rather than being gathered into a sentence condemning her to only one possible life.

The light changed. She walked forward.

# Sources and Limits of Interpretation

This book consists primarily of original contemporary narratives. The materials below locate passages in the classical text, check the attribution of stories, and remind readers of the limits of interpretation. Source labels S1 to S9 correspond to the bracketed markers in the main text; their numbering does not indicate the weight of research findings. Web access date recorded in the Chinese source: November 3, 2026.

Read the classical text and modern interpretations separately. This book does not reproduce modern Chinese translations, present its modern characters as the actual experiences of ancient figures, or use philosophical fables as experimental evidence of psychological benefits or social laws. All contemporary stories are original fiction. What ancient figures say and do in fables is not equivalent to directly verifiable biographical fact.
